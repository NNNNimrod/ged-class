How to Identify and Classify a Polynomial

A step-by-step guide to naming a polynomial by counting its terms. There's nothing to solve here, only something to look at carefully and name.

General class material (not assigned to a student) · Skill tag: [MATH | A.1 | MP.4.b | DOK 1 | FIB | NA | CCR:E] (expressions and polynomials, foundation for A.1.d–A.1.g)

Our example for the whole page: \(4y^2 - 9\). By the end you'll be able to say: "\(4y^2 - 9\) has two terms, so it's a binomial (a two-term polynomial)."

1Know what a term is

A term is one "chunk" of an expression. It can be:

  • a number by itself, like \(8\)
  • a letter (variable) by itself, like \(k\)
  • numbers and letters multiplied together, like \(4y^2\) or \(3a^2b\)

In \(4y^2 - 9\), the chunks are \(4y^2\) and \(9\) (with its sign, as you'll see in Step 3).

Why it works

Think of terms like items in a shopping list. Each item is one thing, even if it has a few words, like "large red apples". A term can have several parts stuck together by multiplication and still be just one item.

2Check that it's a polynomial at all

A polynomial is one or more terms added or subtracted, where every exponent on a variable is a whole number (0, 1, 2, 3, …).

ExpressionPolynomial?
\(4y^2 - 9\)Yes, the exponents are whole numbers
\(\dfrac{y}{2} + 1\)Yes, dividing by a number is fine
\(\sqrt{16} + k\)Yes, \(\sqrt{16}\) is just the number 4
\(\dfrac{3}{x} + 1\)No, there's a variable in the denominator
\(\sqrt{x} + 2\)No, there's a variable under a root
\(x^{-2} + 4\)No, it has a negative exponent

Why it works

\(\dfrac{3}{x}\) is the same as \(3x^{-1}\), and \(\sqrt{x}\) is the same as \(x^{1/2}\). Those exponents aren't whole numbers, so they break the rule.

A plain number under a root, like \(\sqrt{16}\), is fine because it's just a constant, the number 4.

3Split at every + and − sign

The plus and minus signs between chunks are where you cut. Each sign stays glued to the term after it.

\(+4y^2\)\(-9\)

Term 1 (blue): \(+4y^2\)  ·  Term 2 (red): \(-9\)

The first term has no sign written, so it's positive.

Why it works

Subtracting 9 is the same as adding negative 9: \(4y^2 - 9 = 4y^2 + (-9)\). So the minus sign is really part of the second term. Keeping signs attached matters later, when you combine or move terms.

4Don't split inside a term

Multiplying, exponents and dividing by a number all happen inside a term. They never cut it apart.

\(3a^2b\) → one term (3 times a times a times b)
\(4y^2\) → one term (4 times y times y)
\(\dfrac{y}{2}\) → one term (y divided by 2)

Why it works

Only adding and subtracting separate terms. The little 2 in \(4y^2\) means \(4 \cdot y \cdot y\), which is multiplication, so it stays in one piece.

5Count numbers by themselves too

A number with no letter is called a constant, and it counts as a term.

\(4y^2\)\(-9\) ← the \(-9\) counts!

A constant alone, like \(8\), is a polynomial with one term.

Why it works

It's called a constant because its value never changes, no matter what \(y\) is. It's still one chunk of the expression, so it gets counted like any other term.

6Count the terms, then name it

Count the colored chunks: \(4y^2 - 9\) has 2 terms.

Number of termsNameExample
1monomial\(5k\)
2binomial\(4y^2 - 9\)
3trinomial\(-6m^3 + 2m - 7\)

Why it works

The start of the word tells you the number:
mono = 1 (like a monorail, one rail)
bi = 2 (like a bicycle, two wheels)
tri = 3 (like a tricycle, three wheels)
With 4 or more terms, just call it a polynomial.

7Watch out: count it as written

Look at \(5x^2 + 2x^2\). You could simplify it to \(7x^2\), but this question asks about the expression as it's written.

\(+5x^2\)\(+2x^2\) → 2 terms → binomial

Why it works

Your book counts the terms you can see. Don't combine like terms before you count unless the question tells you to. Simplifying first changes the expression, so you'd be naming a different one.

8Put it all together

  • Is it a polynomial? Yes, the only exponent is 2, a whole number.
  • Split at the signs: \(4y^2\) \(-9\)
  • Count: 2 terms.

\(4y^2 - 9\) has two terms, so it's a binomial (a two-term polynomial).

Why it works

Every time, follow the same order: check → split → count → name. Doing the steps the same way each time keeps you from skipping a sign or a constant.

Bonus (optional): the highest exponent

You don't need this to name a polynomial by its terms. If you're curious, look at every exponent and find the biggest one. In \(4y^2 - 9\), the biggest exponent is 2. In \(-6m^3 + 2m - 7\), it's 3. Some books call this number the degree.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the constant. \(4y^2 - 9\) has 2 terms, not 1. The \(-9\) counts.
  • Splitting at multiplication. \(3a^2b\) is one term, not three.
  • Thinking an exponent means more terms. The 2 in \(4y^2\) doesn't make two terms.
  • Simplifying before counting. \(5x^2 + 2x^2\) is a binomial as written.
  • Dropping the sign. The second term of \(4y^2 - 9\) is \(-9\), not \(9\).
